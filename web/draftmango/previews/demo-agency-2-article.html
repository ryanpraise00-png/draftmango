The 90-day plan we give every new client.

Strategy · 8 min read

Week by week: the audit, the quick wins, the tests worth running and the single report we send on day 90.

Every new client asks the same question on the first call: what happens in the first three months? For years our answer was a slide. Now it is a plan with dates on it, and it is the same plan for a coffee subscription and a cybersecurity platform, because the order of the work matters more than the channel mix.

This is that plan, week by week. It is not a promise of results by a date. It is a promise about how the time will be spent, and about what you will have in your hands when it is up.

Weeks 1–2: The audit

Nothing launches in the first fortnight. We read before we write: ad accounts, analytics, the CRM, search console and the last twelve months of email. Most of it is unglamorous work, and most of the money we later find comes from it.

What we look for first

Tracking comes before everything else. In roughly half of the accounts we inherit, conversions are counted twice, missing a key event or credited to the wrong channel. Until that is fixed, every number in the audit is a guess, so the fix is usually the first thing we ship.

  • Spend going to campaigns with no measurable return.
  • Landing pages that lose most visitors before the first scroll.
  • What a customer is actually worth after twelve months, not after one order.
  • Questions people search for that the site never answers.

The audit ends in a meeting, not a PDF. We walk through the findings with whoever signs the budget, and we agree on the three numbers we will be judged by.

Weeks 2–4: The roadmap

The roadmap starts before the audit is finished, because by the second week the shape of the problem is usually clear. We list every idea the team and the client can think of, then score each one on impact, confidence and effort. Most ideas do not survive the scoring, which is the point.

What survives becomes a sequence, not a list. Each test has an owner, a start date and a single number that tells us whether it worked. Quick wins go first, not because they are big, but because they pay for the patience the larger bets need.

Weeks 4–8: The launch

By week four the first changes are live: the tracking fixes, budget moved away from the campaigns that were not earning it, and the landing page rewrite the audit flagged. Then the test calendar starts, at two or three experiments a week across channels.

One change at a time, per channel

Tests run in parallel across channels, but never stacked on the same page or audience. When a landing page test and a new bidding strategy go live on the same day, nobody can say which one moved the number, and the result teaches you nothing.

Expect the first fortnight of the launch to look noisy. Algorithms relearn, a few tests lose and the dashboard swings. We say this out loud at kickoff, so nobody panics in week five.

Weeks 9–13: Scale what worked

The second half of the quarter is about concentration. Winning tests become the new baseline, the budget follows them, and the losers are written up and retired. This is also when the slower channels, search and lifecycle, show their first real numbers.

Before we raise a budget

  • The winning test has run for at least two full buying cycles.
  • The lift holds for new customers, not only returning ones.
  • Margin after ad cost stays above the floor we agreed in week two.
  • The landing page can carry twice the traffic without slowing down.

If any of those is a no, the budget waits. Scaling a result that is not real is the fastest way we know to lose a quarter of progress in a month.

Where the slow channels fit

Search and lifecycle rarely win the first month. A rewritten category page needs weeks to be crawled, ranked and cited in AI answers, and a welcome flow needs a few hundred new subscribers before its numbers mean anything. We start both in week four, so they mature just as paid media reaches its ceiling. By week twelve they usually carry a third of the new revenue.

Day 90: One report

On day ninety we send one document, about six pages long. It answers four questions: what we changed, what it did to the three numbers we agreed in week two, what surprised us, and what we would do with the next ninety days.

The three numbers, before and after

Every report opens with the same table: the three numbers we agreed in week two, where they stood on day one, where they stand now, and how sure we are that our work caused the change. When a number went the wrong way, it stays in the table with a sentence on why. Clients trust the green rows because the red ones are never hidden.

There is no appendix of vanity charts. If a metric did not move the business, it does not make the report. Most clients forward it to their board unedited, which is the only review of our reporting we have ever needed.

Then the next ninety days begin, and the plan gets shorter, because the audit is already done.
