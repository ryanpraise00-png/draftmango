Ten floors, one concrete frame: how the house was built.

A 1960s office block, three years and a lot of oak. What we kept, what we cut away and why every room got a window to the floor.

Henrique CaladoCo-founder · ran the build

When we first walked through the building in 2021 it was ten floors of suspended ceilings, grey carpet and strip lights, empty since an insurance company moved out. What it also had was a frame of reinforced concrete poured in 1964, sound from the foundations to the roof, and a view down the Avenida that nobody had looked at in decades.

Our rule from the start: keep the frame, take away everything that hid it, and give every room the window the old offices never had. That rule cost us a year of structural work and is the reason the house feels the way it does.

Keeping the bones

Demolishing and starting again would have been quicker and, on paper, cheaper. But the frame held 1,900 tonnes of concrete that we did not have to pour again, and the old floor heights gave us ceilings of three metres. We stripped it back to the columns, cut new openings where the windows go to the floor, and left the board-marked concrete bare in the lobby, where you can still see the grain of the 1964 shuttering.

The best material in the building was the one that was already there. We just had to stop covering it up.

Henrique Calado

Fins, oak and glass

The facade is new: pale concrete fins, spaced to keep the low summer sun off the glass and let the winter sun in. Inside, the architects at Estúdio Vale Morais kept to four materials only: smoked oak from a mill in the north, linen woven in Guimarães, black steel and a warm grey stone for the bathrooms. The rooftop pool sits on a new steel table above the old roof slab, so its weight never touches the 1964 frame.

What it took

  • Three years, from the first drawing to the first guest in May 2025
  • 1,900 tonnes of the original concrete frame, kept
  • 212 new windows, every one of them to the floor
  • Solar panels on the plant room and heat recovered from the pool for the showers

If you stay with us, take the stairs down from the lobby once. Run your hand along the concrete wall and you will feel the boards of 1964.

Henrique CaladoCo-founder of Draftmango House

Henrique trained as a structural engineer and spent ten years on bridges before he and Marta started Draftmango House. He still walks the building every Monday with the maintenance team.
