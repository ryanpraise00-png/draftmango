Changeovers are a management problem, not a machine problem.

Sixty percent of lost changeover time is waiting. Here is how three food plants took 38% off every switch without buying a machine.

Ask a plant manager why changeovers take so long and you will hear about the machines. Time a hundred of them and a different story appears: people waiting.

In the Harlow Foods diagnostic we timed 160 changeovers across nine lines. The average took seventy minutes. Forty-two of those minutes were spent waiting for something: a mechanic, a part, a size kit, or a quality sign-off at the end.

Waiting is a scheduling decision

Nobody chooses to wait. But every wait has an owner, and in most plants that owner is not in the room when the line stops. The mechanic is on another line. The size kit is in a cage that opens at seven. Quality signs off when the technician finishes the previous check.

“We did not need a faster machine. We needed the right person to arrive before the line stopped.”

Megan Doyle, COO, Harlow Foods

Three changes that cost nothing

The fix was ordinary, which is why it held. Parts and tools were staged an hour before each planned switch. The mechanic's day was planned around the changeover schedule instead of the other way round. Quality agreed a first-off check the operators could run themselves.

What to try on Monday

  • Time ten changeovers with a stopwatch and write down every wait
  • Give each wait an owner, by name
  • Stage the next changeover's kit before the current run ends
  • Review the times every morning for a month, then decide on machines

Within fourteen weeks the average changeover at Harlow fell from seventy minutes to forty-three. One of two approved equipment projects was cancelled. The other was rescoped at half the cost.

Claire Ashdown

Partner, Operations. Ran two food plants before joining Draftmango in 2013.
