The harbor wall held. Now comes the harder choice.

Last week's king tide was the first real test of the $1.1 billion barrier. Engineers say it passed, and that the harder choices start now.

By Celia Renner March 11, 2027 · 9 min read

At 7:42 on Thursday morning, the water outside the ferry terminal stood 9.6 feet above the average low tide, the highest reading since the city began keeping records in 1912. For the first time, there was a wall in its way.

The barrier, two miles of concrete and steel along the harbor front, closed its four gates at 5 a.m. and held for seven hours. Behind it, the promenade stayed dry enough for commuters to walk to work. A decade ago, the same tide would have flooded 3,000 basements.

“It did exactly what we designed it to do,” said Mareike Dorn, the city's chief resilience engineer, standing on the wall's north gate the next morning. “That's the good news. The harder news is what it can't do.”

Built for 40 years

The wall was designed for a sea level 1.6 feet higher than today's, the city's planning figure for 2065. Beyond that, the engineers say, it can be raised once, by about two feet, before the foundations reach their limit.

That leaves the city a window of roughly four decades to decide what comes next: a second, taller barrier further out in the bay, a managed retreat from the lowest blocks, or both.

The wall buys us time. It doesn't buy us a plan.

Mareike Dorn, chief resilience engineer

Neither option is cheap. A preliminary study, published by the port authority in January, puts an outer barrier at $4.8 billion. Buying out and relocating the 1,100 households in the lowest zone would cost about $900 million, according to the same study, but would take a political consensus the city has never had.

What the tide gauge says

The gauge at Pier 9 has recorded the harbor every six minutes for more than a century. Its readings show the sea here rising about 0.18 inches a year, close to twice the global average, because the land beneath the old port is also slowly sinking.

Council members will hear the first options at a public session on April 2. Dorn says she would rather the city argue about it now. “Forty years sounds like a long time,” she said. “For a project this size, it's about two council terms of planning and three of building.”

On the promenade, the morning after the tide, the commuters had already stopped looking at the water.

Climate Correspondent

Celia Renner

Celia has covered the harbor, the weather and the people who plan for both since 2019. She grew up two blocks from the old ferry terminal.
