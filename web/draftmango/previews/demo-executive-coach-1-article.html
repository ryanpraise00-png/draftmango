The first 100 days in a C-suite role.

Most new executives arrive with a plan for the business and none for themselves. Amelia Hart on the three months that set the shape of the next three years.

Amelia HartCo-founder and Head of Faculty

Article28 Sep 2026·7 min read

In this article

  1. Before day one
  2. Days 1–30: listen
  3. Days 31–60: decide what to stop
  4. Days 61–100: show the shape of the year
  5. Questions to sit with

In our 2026 Leadership Pulse, six in ten new executives told us their first ninety days had no plan. Not for the business: most had a strategy deck before they signed. No plan for themselves, for how they would learn the place, earn the trust of a team they had not chosen, and decide what to stop doing.

I have coached new chief executives and chief operating officers for eighteen years, and ran operations for eleven before that. The first hundred days are rarely lost to a bad decision. They are lost to a full diary.

Before day one

Write down three things you believe about the business, and keep the page. In week twelve you will read it again, and the gap between that page and what you know by then is the most useful thing you will learn all year.

Agree with your chair or chief executive what success looks like at day one hundred. Not the results: the conversations you will have had, and the two or three calls you will have made.

Days 1–30: listen

Book forty conversations in the first month. Your team, their teams, three customers, the people who applied for your job, the person who left it. Ask each of them the same three questions:

  • What should I keep exactly as it is?
  • What is everyone here too polite to tell me?
  • If you had my job, what would you do first?

“The first hundred days are rarely lost to a bad decision. They are lost to a full diary.”

Days 31–60: decide what to stop

New leaders add. They add meetings to learn, reports to see, reviews to feel in control. By week six the diary has no white space and the team has learned that every decision goes through you.

Make a list of everything that comes to your desk in a fortnight. Then give a third of it away, by name, in writing. One client of ours cancelled four standing meetings in a single afternoon. Nobody missed them.

Days 61–100: show the shape of the year

By now you know more than you did, and less than you will. That is the right moment to say out loud what you have heard, what you will change, and what you will leave alone. Teams forgive a leader who changes direction; they struggle with one who never tells them where they are going.

Take it to your team first, then to the board. And keep it to one page: three priorities, what each one will cost, and who owns it.

Questions to sit with.

  1. Who in this organisation knows something I need, and has not been asked?
  2. Which meeting in my diary exists because I am new, not because it is useful?
  3. What would my team say I want, if I left the room?
  4. What will I have stopped doing by day one hundred?
