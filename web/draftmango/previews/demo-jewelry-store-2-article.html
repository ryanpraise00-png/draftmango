Choosing a lab-grown diamond: what the report really tells you

Simon FairleighCo-founder, Stones & Client Desk · October 20, 2026

Every week someone sits across the table from me with a grading report in one hand and a worried look. The report is useful, but it lists everything with the same weight, and most of it matters far less than the first line.

Here is how I read one, in the order I read it, using the stone in our Aube Oval Solitaire as the example. Everything below applies to mined diamonds too; a lab-grown diamond is the same carbon crystal, it simply took months to grow rather than ages.

01Start with the cut, and stop if it is not excellent

Cut is the only grade a person makes. It describes how well the facets send light back to your eye, and a beautifully coloured, flawless stone with a poor cut still looks glassy. We only set stones graded Excellent; on fancy shapes like ovals, ask about the bow-tie, the dark band across the middle that the report does not grade.

At the tableWe look at every oval in daylight by the window and under a desk lamp: a bow-tie shows in one and hides in the other.

02Colour: D to G is enough

Colour runs from D, completely colourless, down the alphabet towards a warm tint. Once a stone sits in yellow or rose gold, nobody can tell a D from a G across a table, and the difference in price is real. In white gold or platinum we suggest staying at F or above.

03Clarity: what you cannot see does not count

Clarity grades the tiny inclusions inside the crystal, as seen under ten times magnification. From VS2 upwards they are invisible to the eye in any normal light. Paying for VVS buys a better report, not a better-looking ring.

04Carat is weight, not size

Two stones of 1.2 carats can look quite different on the hand. Long shapes spread their weight: an oval, a pear or a marquise faces up larger than a round of the same carat, and a deep stone hides its weight underneath. Ask for the millimetre size, then try it on.

Choose the cut first, the shape second and the size last. The report will agree with you, and so will your hand.

Simon Fairleigh

05The line most people skip

At the end of every report is the inscription: a number laser-written on the girdle, too small to see without a loupe. It ties the stone to its paper for life. When your ring comes in for its Lifetime Care visit, we read it back to you before it goes home.
