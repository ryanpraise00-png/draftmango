Counting the Carbon in Concrete Before the First Pour

Sofia MarchettiAssociate Director, Sustainability · London
Share

Half of a tower's embodied carbon is poured into the ground before anyone sees it. For years we counted it at the end. Now we count it at the first sketch.

On Meridian Tower the foundations, core and floor plates held 58% of the building's embodied carbon. None of that was visible in the renderings that won the competition, and almost none of it was on the table when the big decisions were made.

Count it while it can still change

Every option in our models now carries three numbers side by side: floor area, cost and carbon. A deeper basement, a thicker slab, a longer span — each shows its carbon the moment it is drawn, so the conversation with the engineer happens in week two, not month twenty.

“The cheapest tonne of carbon is the one you decide not to pour.”

What the mix can do

Cement is the culprit, not the concrete. Replacing part of it with slag or fly ash, and asking for strength at 56 days instead of 28, changes the number more than any other single decision on site.

And then use less of it

The mix is half the story. Shorter spans, post-tensioned slabs and a timber frame above the podium took another fifth out of Meridian's structure. The result: 31% less embodied carbon than a benchmark tower of the same size.

Key Takeaways
  • Count carbon beside cost and area from the first sketch.
  • Specify strength at 56 days so half the cement can go.
  • Shorter spans and lighter frames beat any single material swap.
