Naming is the first design decision

(Branding · 7 min read)

Why we start every identity project with words, not logos, and the four tests a name has to pass.

Julien MoreauFounder & Creative Director

Sep 24, 20267 min read

Share

Contents

Words First The Four Tests The Sprint Common Mistakes Then the Logo

Words come before logos

Every identity project we take on starts the same way: a long table, a stack of blank cards and no pencils for drawing. Before anyone opens a design file, we spend a week on the name, or on the words around a name the client already has.

It can look like a detour. Founders arrive hoping to see a logo, and we hand them a spreadsheet. But a name sets the tone, the tempo and the promise of everything that follows. A soft, two-syllable word asks for a different typeface than a hard, clipped one. A name that sounds like a person invites warmth; a name that sounds like an instrument invites precision.

Draw first and you end up decorating a word you never chose on purpose. Get the word right and half the design brief writes itself.

The four tests a name must pass

Long lists are easy. Shortlists are where the work is. Every candidate we present has survived four tests, in this order, and most fall at the first one.

Sayable

Read it aloud to someone who has never seen it written down, then ask them to spell it. If they hesitate, people will hesitate on the phone, on a podcast and across a noisy room. A name that can’t travel by voice won’t travel far.

Ownable

Can you hold the domain, the handles and the trademark in the markets that matter? Just as important: does it sound like your three closest competitors? A name that blends into its category is cheap to register and expensive to make famous.

Stretchable

Picture the company in five years, with two new products and an office in another country. Does the name still fit, or does it pin you to the thing you sell today? We prefer names that carry a feeling or a stance over names that describe a feature.

True

The last test is the quiet one. Does the word hold something the team actually believes? Halcyra wanted calm, not clinical, so the name had to sound like a slow breath before it meant anything at all. Names that bend the truth get found out by the first customer.

A name you have to explain is a name you’ll pay to explain, every single day.

Julien Moreau, on the first test

How a naming sprint runs

Our sprints last two weeks and keep the same rhythm, whether the brief is a bakery or a bank.

  1. Territory. We agree on three or four directions, warm, technical or playful, before writing a single candidate.
  2. Volume. The team writes alone first, then together, until each direction holds more than a hundred words.
  3. Shortlist. Every candidate faces the four tests. Usually eight to twelve survive.
  4. Context. We set the finalists on a shop front, an app icon and an email signature, because a word on a white slide tells you very little.
  5. Decision. The founders choose, and legal clearance comes before anyone falls in love.

The context step changes more minds than any argument. A word that felt bold on a list can look shy on a bus shelter, and a plain one can suddenly glow on an app icon.

Mistakes we see most

Naming by committee. Twelve people voting will always land on the least objectionable word. Give the decision to two or three people and let everyone else advise.

Falling for the domain first. A free .com is a bonus, not a reason. A great name with a small prefix beats a weak name with a perfect address.

Explaining instead of evoking. Descriptive names feel safe on day one and dull by year two. Let the tagline explain, and let the name be remembered.

Testing in a vacuum. Polls ask people what they like, not what they remember. Show a name once, wait a day, then ask what stuck.

Where the logo comes in

Once the word is chosen, design moves fast. The rhythm of the letters suggests a typeface, the meaning suggests a colour, and the story behind it hands the first campaign its line. On Tallyfern, the name arrived before the mark, and the mark took three days instead of three weeks.

So when a client asks why we open with a spreadsheet, the answer is short: the name is the first thing everyone will design around, so it deserves to be designed too.
