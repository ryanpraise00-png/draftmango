Why a logo is the last thing we draw.

Clients come to us for a logo. We make them wait for it — and by the time we draw it, it nearly draws itself.

Freja HolmFounder & Creative Director

Oct 8, 2026·5 min read

Most briefs we receive open with the same sentence: “We need a new logo.” It’s a fair thing to ask for. The logo is the part of a brand everyone can point at — the bit that ends up on the door, the box and the browser tab. So when we say we’ll draw it last, around week six of twelve, the first reaction is usually polite confusion.

Here’s the reason. A logo drawn first has to carry every decision nobody has made yet. It becomes the place where strategy, voice and colour all get argued about at once, and a small drawing can’t hold that much weight. Drawn last, it only has to say one thing, and say it well.

What comes before the pencil

The first five weeks of a Draftmango project look nothing like design from the outside. We interview the founders, the people who answer the phone and a handful of customers. We read every piece of copy the company has ever published. We write a one-page brief that everyone signs, and only then does anyone open a sketchbook.

By the time the drawing starts, four things are settled, in this order:

  1. What the brand stands for, in one sentence a customer could repeat.
  2. How it speaks — three words for the voice, and three it must never use.
  3. Its colour: a lead, a support and a signal, with the proportions written down.
  4. Its type: one face for names and headlines, one for everything else.

Each of those narrows the field. A brand that speaks plainly won’t get an ornate monogram. A palette led by a deep fern won’t sit under a mark that only works in black. By week six the sketchbook isn’t a blank page any more; it’s a room with the walls already up.

Waiting also changes the conversation in the room. When the first sketches go up on the wall, nobody asks whether they like the green, because the green was agreed a fortnight ago. The only question left is whether the mark fits what we already know — and that’s a question a team can actually answer.

It saves money, too. In the projects we’ve inherited from other studios, a logo chosen in week one was redrawn twice before launch on average — once when the strategy landed and once when the colour did. Drawing it last means drawing it once, and spending the saved weeks on the parts of a brand people use every day: templates, signs and packaging.

How the mark gets drawn

When we do draw, we draw a lot. Fieldnote’s leaf went through more than sixty pencil versions before two went up on the pin-up wall. We test each one at the sizes it will really live at: sixteen pixels in a browser tab, five millimetres on a pencil, two metres on a shop sign. Most of them die at sixteen pixels.

The ones that live face a second test: colour. Each candidate is printed in the lead colour on the brand’s own paper, reversed out of the signal colour, and in plain black on a cheap photocopy. A mark that needs its colour to be recognised isn’t finished yet.

The survivors get built properly. We put each mark on a construction plate — the circles, tangents and clear space that make it repeatable — so the next designer, at the client or at a printer, can redraw it exactly. That plate goes into the brand book on page four, right after the strategy.

Then we stop. A finished mark is quiet. It doesn’t need to explain the company, because the voice, the colour and the type around it are already doing that work. The best compliment we get is when a client says the logo feels as if it was always there.

Clients who have been through it rarely ask for the logo first the second time. Ida Berg at Fieldnote put it better than we can: the leaf was the easiest decision of the whole project, because by then everything else was already true. If a logo is hard to choose, it usually means something earlier hasn’t been decided.

So yes, we’ll draw your logo. We’ll just do it last. If you want to see where it led for Fieldnote, the whole story is in the case study.
