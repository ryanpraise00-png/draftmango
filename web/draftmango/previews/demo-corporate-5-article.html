The board’s first 100 days after a deal

The deal is signed and the advisers go home. For the board, the hardest part has just begun.

Victoria AshfordSenior Partner · September 18, 2026

Most of the energy in a deal goes into the months before signing. The model, the price, the diligence, the vote. Then the bankers leave, the lawyers file, and the board meets again to find that the company it now governs is not quite the one in the model.

The first hundred days decide whether the deal pays for itself. They are also the days when boards are most tempted to step back and let management get on with it.

Ask for three numbers, every month

Not the full integration report. Three numbers: the synergies booked against the plan, the people you meant to keep and did, and the customers you meant to keep and did. If management cannot give you those three by day thirty, that is your first finding.

A board that waits for the first annual review to ask about integration has already lost a year.

Meet the acquired team without the acquirer

Once, early, and with the chief executive's blessing. The people who came with the deal know where the value is, and they will tell a director things they will not tell their new boss.

Decide in advance when you will step in

Write it down before day one: the numbers, and the dates, at which the board will ask for a different plan. Boards that agree their triggers in advance act on them. Boards that do not, argue about them.

None of this is complicated. It is simply easier to skip when the deal has just closed and everyone is tired. That is precisely why it matters.
