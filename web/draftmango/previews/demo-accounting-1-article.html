Why your January tax bill is bigger than you think.

Your first Self Assessment bill often comes with a second one attached. Here's why, and how to plan for both.

Hannah Brook Chartered Tax Adviser

Published
8 October 2026
Topic
Self Assessment
Reading Time
6 min
Share

Every January, someone calls us expecting a tax bill of around six thousand pounds and opens one for nine. Nothing has gone wrong. They have met payments on account.

It's one of the least loved parts of Self Assessment, mostly because nobody explains it until the money is due. So let's explain it now, while there's still time to plan.

What a payment on account is

If your last Self Assessment bill was more than £1,000, HMRC asks you to pay towards next year's bill in advance. It splits the estimate into two halves, each worth half of last year's bill.

The first half is due on 31 January, the second on 31 July. Think of it as HMRC moving you onto instalments, whether you asked or not.

Why the first January hurts

In your first year, January brings two things at once: the bill for the year just gone, and the first payment towards the year you're in now.

After the first year it evens out. Each January you settle any difference and make the next payment, and the numbers stop jumping.

“The tax isn't extra. It's the same tax, just asked for earlier.”

If your income is falling

Payments on account assume this year looks like last year. If you know it won't, you can ask HMRC to reduce them. We do this for clients most years, usually after a quieter spring.

Be careful, though. If you reduce them too far and the year turns out well, HMRC charges interest on the difference.

How to stay ahead

  • Put 25–30% of every payment you receive into a separate tax pot
  • File in the autumn, so you know the number months early
  • Ask for a reduction only when you're confident
  • Set two reminders: 31 January and 31 July

Or let us keep an eye on it. Our clients see their next bill in the app, with a tax pot that fills up as they go.
