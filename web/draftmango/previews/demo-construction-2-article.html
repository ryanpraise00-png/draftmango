What a written estimate should include

Seven things to look for before you compare two contractors' numbers.

This Post

Written by
Rachel Moore
Published
Oct 8, 2026
Topic
Planning
Reading time
7 minutes

Two estimates for the same kitchen can be $20,000 apart and both be honest. The difference is almost always in what each one leaves out. After writing about four hundred of them a year, here is what I would look for in anyone's estimate, including ours.

1. A scope you could hand to another builder

Every room, every wall that moves, every fixture that is replaced or kept. If the scope fits on one page, the price is a guess. Ours run from eight to twenty pages for a renovation.

2. Allowances with names and numbers

Tile, lighting, plumbing fixtures and appliances are usually chosen later. A good estimate names each one with its amount: primary bath tile, $4,200 installed. A low allowance is the easiest way to make an estimate look cheap.

3. Permits, fees and inspections

Who pulls the permit, what it costs and how many inspections the job needs. In Williamson County a kitchen with a moved wall usually means three.

4. What happens when we open a wall

Old houses hide rot, knob-and-tube wiring and the odd beam that is not there. The estimate should say how a surprise is priced: a written change order, signed before the extra work starts.

“A low allowance is the easiest way to make an estimate look cheap.”

5. A schedule with two dates

A start date and a finish date, and roughly when each trade is in the house. Without them, “eight to ten weeks” has no starting line.

6. Payments tied to work, not to the calendar

A deposit at signing, then draws when stages are finished: rough-ins passed, cabinets set, walk-through done. Never most of the money before the walls are closed.

7. The warranty, in writing

What is covered, for how long and who you call. Ours is ten years on structure and two on finishes, with Kate Sullivan's number on the first page.
