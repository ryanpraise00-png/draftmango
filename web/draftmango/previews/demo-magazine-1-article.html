Cover Story Issue 42 · March 14, 2027 · 14 min read

In praise of the unhurried life.

Twelve people who traded the early alarm for long lunches, small gardens and weekdays that feel like Sundays, and what a slower year taught them about time.

Words by Margot RavelPhotographs by Petra Bellamy

Share

The first thing Ana Keswick did when she left her agency job in Chicago was buy a second chair for the kitchen. Not a better chair, she is quick to say, just a second one, so that whoever dropped by in the morning could stay for the length of a pot of coffee. Three years later, breakfast in her house in Hudson, New York, runs from eight until nearly eleven, and the chair is rarely empty.

She is one of twelve people we followed through a year for this issue: a baker, two teachers, a retired surgeon, a couple who run a twelve-room inn, a potter, a ferry captain. None of them are rich, and none of them would call themselves minimalists. What they share is a decision, made at some point in the last decade, to stop measuring a day by how much of it was used.

The four-hour lunch

In a valley outside Lucca, six friends have met for lunch every Saturday since 2019. The rules are short: no phones on the table, everyone brings one dish, and nobody leaves before the coffee. "It started as a joke about how long Italians take to eat," says Teo Bianchi, who cooks for the group most weeks. "Now it is the only meeting in my calendar I have never once moved."

Long meals, it turns out, are the most common thread in the year we spent reporting this story. Ten of the twelve described one, unprompted, as the moment their week felt most like their own.

“We used to ask how the week went. Now we ask what the week was like. It is a different question.”Ana Keswick, Hudson, New York

Mornings in Hudson: breakfast, the bean rows, and the window seat Ana calls “the office.” Photographs by Petra Bellamy.

What a slower year costs

Nobody we spoke to pretended the change was free. The surgeon took a 40 percent pay cut to work four days a week. The innkeepers close for all of January, which means a thin February. Ana now earns about half of what she did at the agency, and has learned to fix her own bicycle.

What they bought with it is harder to put in a spreadsheet, though several of them tried. The most precise answer came from the ferry captain, who keeps a notebook of every evening he has spent on his porch since he stopped taking the late crossing: 212 last year, against 31 the year before.

Three small things they all do

  • Eat at least one meal a week that lasts longer than it needs to.
  • Keep one day with nothing written in the calendar, on purpose.
  • Walk somewhere they could have driven.

On our last morning in Hudson, the second chair had a visitor before eight: a neighbor returning a borrowed casserole dish, who stayed until the coffee ran out. Ana did not look at the clock once. We did, and it was ten past eleven.
