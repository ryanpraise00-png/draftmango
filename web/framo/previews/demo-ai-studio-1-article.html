Write a prompt like a shot list.

Subject, light, lens, mood. Four lines that turn a vague wish into a frame you can repeat.

Leo HartmannHead of Craft · 14 October 2026

Tutorial12 min read

Most prompts fail the same way: they describe a feeling and hope the picture follows. A shot list does the opposite. It names what the camera sees, in the order a crew would set it up.

We have read a few million prompts by now. The ones that land on the first take share a shape, and it is the same shape a photographer uses on a call sheet. Four lines, written plainly, each with one job.

Start with the subject

Say what the frame is about before anything else, in words a stranger could picture. “A crowd under a violet light wave” beats “energy, music, night vibes”. Muse 3 gives the first few words the most weight, so spend them on the thing you would point the camera at.

Then light it

Light is the line most people skip, and the one that changes the most. Name where it comes from and how warm it is: “low sun from the left”, “one bare bulb”, “overcast, no shadows”. If you only add one thing to your prompts this week, add this.

Same bay, one word apart: “low sun behind the headland” and “flat noon light, from above”.

Choose a lens

You don’t need to know cameras to use lens words. Think of them as distance. A 24mm puts you inside the room; an 85mm lets you watch from across it. “Macro” goes close enough to see the grain of things.

A good prompt reads like a call sheet, not a poem. Save the poetry for the last line.

End on the mood

One or two words, at the end, where they color everything without steering the composition. “Quiet”, “tense”, “late summer”. More than two and they start to argue with each other.

When a take is close

  1. Lock the seed, so the composition stays put.
  2. Change one line at a time, and only one word in it.
  3. When it lands, render it again in Muse 3 at full size.

The short version

Subject firstSay where the light isLens as distanceMood last
