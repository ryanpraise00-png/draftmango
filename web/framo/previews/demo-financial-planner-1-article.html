Roth conversions in a low-income year: who should consider one

A sabbatical, a gap between jobs or the first years after you stop working can be the cheapest time you'll ever have to move money into a Roth.

Theo Faulkner, EATax Planning Lead

Theo Faulkner, EATax Planning Lead

Reviewed by Maya Abbott, CFP®. General education, not individual tax advice.

Thinking about a conversion?

Talk it through, free

Every dollar in a pre-tax retirement account will be taxed one day. A Roth conversion lets you choose the day, and some years that day is unusually cheap.

What a Roth conversion is

You move money from a pre-tax account, such as a traditional IRA or an old 401(k), into a Roth IRA. The amount you move counts as income for the year and is taxed at your normal rates. After that it grows tax-free, and qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free too.

Nothing about the investments has to change. It's the tax wrapper that moves, not the portfolio.

Why the year matters

A conversion is taxed at your marginal rate in the year you make it. In a normal working year that may be 24% or higher. In a low-income year it can be 12% or less: a sabbatical, a gap between jobs, a year of parental leave, or the first years after you stop working and before Social Security and required withdrawals begin.

The cheapest tax you'll ever pay on retirement money is often in a year that feels like a pause.

A worked example

Hypothetical

A couple, both 41, take a year off to travel. Their income for the year is $38,000 from freelance work. They convert $60,000 from an old 401(k).

Federal tax on the conversion this year
≈ $7,000
The same $60,000 in a working year at 24%
≈ $14,400
Difference, paid from savings, not the IRA
≈ $7,400

Rounded, federal tax only, before any state tax. Your numbers will differ.

Who should consider one

  • You have a low-income year ahead of you, or one that just ended.
  • You hold large pre-tax balances you'll have to draw from later anyway.
  • You can pay the tax from cash, not from the money you convert.
  • You expect your tax rate in retirement to be the same or higher.
  • You'd like to leave tax-free money to your children.

Who should probably wait

  • You may need the converted money within five years; each conversion has its own five-year clock.
  • You buy health cover on the marketplace, and the extra income would cost you part of your subsidy.
  • You're 63 or older, and the income could raise your Medicare premiums two years later.
  • The only way to pay the tax would be from the IRA itself.

How we decide with you

Before the end of the year we model the conversion inside your plan, bracket by bracket: how much fills the current bracket, what it does to subsidies and premiums, and how it changes the tax you'll pay later. Then we agree on a number, often smaller than people expect, and repeat it in each low-income year.

This article is general education, not individual tax, legal or investment advice. Talk to your planner or tax professional before converting.
