Pricing

Pricing power in a flat market.

When volume won't grow, price is the lever left — and most companies pull it blind.

James WhitfieldPartner, Pricing & Commercial

Published
12 Oct 2026
Reading Time
6 min
Share

Key Takeaways

  • Between list price and cash, the average company we studied lets 16.6% of the price slip away.
  • The largest leak is rarely the discount on the invoice — it is the rebate nobody approved.
  • Repricing works in waves: one category, twelve stores, four weeks, then the next.
  • Price power is a habit run by the business, not a project run by consultants.

For a decade, most of the companies we work with grew their way out of trouble. Volume covered a lot of sins: a discount here, a rebate there, a freight charge quietly absorbed to keep a big customer happy. In a flat market there is nothing to cover them. The sins show up in the margin.

The usual response is a cost programme. It is the wrong place to start. On the 140 companies in our Margin Report, a one-point improvement in price was worth roughly three times a one-point cut in cost — and it took a quarter of the time to land.

Where the price goes

Ask a commercial director what the company charges and you will get the list price. Ask the finance director what the company is paid and you will get a different number. The gap between the two is the pocket-price waterfall, and it is almost always wider than anyone in the room expects.

The striking line is not the discount on the invoice; everyone knows about that. It is the 4.1 points of off-invoice rebates: volume bonuses, marketing funds and year-end payments agreed by account managers, paid by finance, and owned by nobody.

Why the lever stays unpulled

Three things keep companies from using price. They fear losing volume they cannot afford to lose. They price by rule of thumb — cost plus a margin set years ago — across tens of thousands of lines. And nobody owns the whole waterfall: sales owns the list, finance owns the rebates, logistics owns the freight.

You don't need pricing power to raise prices. You need it to stop giving them away.

That is the good news in a flat market. Most of the margin we recover for clients does not come from charging customers more. It comes from charging them what was already agreed, and from asking for something back every time a discount is given.

Four moves that work in a flat year

At Halden Retail we repriced 40,000 lines in four waves and added 310 basis points of EBITDA margin in fourteen months. The method travels well beyond grocery:

  1. Draw the waterfall for every category in three weeks, from invoice data you already hold.
  2. Close the leaks first — unapproved rebates and unpaid terms — before touching a single list price.
  3. Reprice in waves, testing each one in a small set of stores or accounts before rolling it out.
  4. Give one person the waterfall, with a monthly review the leadership team actually attends.

None of this needs a new system. It needs a fact base, a few hard conversations with key accounts, and the patience to do it category by category. The companies that grew margin through 2025 were 3.1 times more likely to have repriced in the twelve months before.

— James Whitfield, London
