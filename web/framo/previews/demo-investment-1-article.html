Why patience is a strategy, not a virtue.

Six years is not a slow hold. It is the time it takes to hire well, buy well and leave a company stronger than we found it.

James Whitfield Partner, Private Equity

Published
14 September 2026
Topic
Outlook
Reading Time
7 min
Share

Every few years someone asks us why we hold companies for so long. The question usually comes with a spreadsheet attached, showing how much better the returns would look if we sold in year three. The spreadsheet is never wrong. It simply measures the wrong thing.

A business does not become more valuable because time passes. It becomes more valuable because people inside it make hundreds of good decisions, most of them small, and each one needs a little room to pay off. A new head of sales needs a year to rebuild a team. A second factory needs two. An acquisition needs three before it feels like one company. None of that fits neatly into an investment memo, and all of it shows up in the price a buyer is willing to pay.

The arithmetic of time

Since 1995 we have held our companies for a little over six years on average. That is longer than most of our peers, and we have been told more than once that it costs us. We do not think it does. A longer hold lets a management team plan for the business rather than for the sale, and it lets us be choosy about who buys it next.

It also changes the conversations we have with founders. When a founder knows we will not be looking for an exit in the second year, they tell us what they really want to build. More often than not, it is something bigger than the plan they came in with.

What six years buys

Patience is not the same as waiting. Every company we own has a plan agreed within its first hundred days, a board that meets monthly and an operating partner who has done the work before. The years are full. They are simply not hurried.

The numbers matter, but the shape behind them matters more. Most of the value in our realised companies was made in years three to six, after the new people had settled and the add-ons had been joined up. Selling earlier would have meant selling a promise. Selling later lets us sell a result.

When to step aside

The hardest part of patience is knowing when it has run its course. We start planning the sale in the first year, not because we are in a hurry, but because the right next owner is worth looking for. When Corvex was sold in 2025, the buyer wanted the team as much as the software. That was the point.

None of this makes us slow. It makes us deliberate. We would rather do fewer things properly and hand on companies that keep growing after we have gone. That, in the end, is the only measure of ownership we trust.

— James Whitfield, London
