Why a story helps you fall asleep.

A voice that asks nothing of you gives a busy mind somewhere to rest.

Nora Ellis Head of Content · 14 October 2026

Science·7 min read

Most nights the trouble is not the body. You are tired enough. It is the mind, replaying the day and rehearsing tomorrow, that keeps the lights on.

A sleep story works by giving that mind something to do which is just interesting enough to follow and far too gentle to worry about. Nothing is at stake. Nobody is in danger. The kettle boils, the ferry crosses, the keeper climbs the stairs.

The twenty minutes before sleep

Sleep researchers call the drift into sleep sleep onset. For most adults it takes ten to twenty minutes, and it goes better when attention is loose. Worry narrows attention onto one point. A slow description of a place widens it again.

That is why our writers avoid plot. There are no twists, no cliffhangers, no names to remember. The best feedback we get is that someone never heard the end.

Why the voice matters

Our narrators read at about a hundred words a minute, a third slower than normal speech. They breathe audibly between sentences. Over the course of a story the pace slows further and the music drops away, so there is no sudden silence to wake you.

The best review a story can get is that nobody remembers how it ends.

Maren Holt, story writer

Making it a habit

A story helps most when it becomes a cue. Play it at the same time, in the same place, and after a week or two your body starts to wind down before the first sentence.

Three things that help

  1. Turn the screen face down once the story starts.
  2. Set the sleep timer, so the voice fades out on its own.
  3. Keep the volume low enough that you have to lean in a little.

If you still lie awake for long stretches most nights, a story is not the whole answer. Talk to your doctor; insomnia is common and treatable.
