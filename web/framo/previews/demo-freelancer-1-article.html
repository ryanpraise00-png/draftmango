Pricing before the brief

I name a number on the first call, before anyone has written a brief. Here is why, and what it does to the eight weeks that follow.

Nora Ashby Independent Designer

Published
Oct 2, 2026
Reading Time
6 minutes
Category
Pricing

Most of the projects I have seen go wrong did not go wrong in the design. They went wrong in week three, when a client who expected to spend six thousand euros opened a proposal for fourteen. Nobody had lied. Nobody had asked, either. The brief was thorough, the workshop was pleasant, and the one fact that decides the shape of everything had been saved for last.

So I do it the other way round. On the first call, usually inside twenty minutes, I say a number out loud.

The number comes first because it is the brief

A budget is not a detail to settle after the creative conversation. It is the creative conversation. Six thousand euros and fourteen thousand euros are not the same project done cheaper or dearer; they are different projects. One is a logo, a palette and a one-page guide. The other has a naming round, a photographer and a website with a content model.

When I hear the budget late, I have already been designing the wrong thing in my head for two weeks. When I hear it early, every question I ask afterwards is a better question.

What I say on the call

I never ask “what is your budget?”. People hear that as a trap, and they answer with a figure a third lower than the true one. I say what things cost here, and let them react:

  • A Brand Sprint is €4,800 and takes two weeks.
  • An identity with its website starts at €9,500 and takes six to ten.
  • A retainer is €3,200 a month for four days of work.

Then I stop talking. The silence that follows is the most useful ten seconds of the project. Some people exhale: they had feared double. Some say it is more than they have, and we talk about what a smaller job would be. Two or three a year say goodbye politely, and we have each saved a fortnight.

“A price said early is a piece of information. A price said late is a negotiation.”

The range, then the quote

The first number is a range, and I say so. After the call I write a one-page brief (theirs, in my words) and attach a fixed quote inside that range. It lists what is included, what is not, and the two things most likely to change the price. Clients sign faster than they used to, I think because nothing on the page is new to them.

The quote is one figure, not a table of hours. Hours invite a client to audit my Tuesdays; a figure invites them to judge the result. If the scope changes in week four, we talk first and the number changes second, in writing, before I draw anything new.

Rule of Thumb

If you cannot say the price in one sentence, the scope is not clear yet. Fix the scope, not the sentence.

What it costs me

Being first with the number has a price of its own. I lose the projects where a longer courtship might have stretched the budget. I sometimes quote low, because twenty minutes is not long to understand a business, and then I keep the quote; that is what fixed means. In eleven years I have eaten the difference four times. Each time was cheaper than one project that ended in an argument about an invoice.

Try it once

If you price your own work, try it on the next enquiry. Say your three numbers before you ask a single question about goals or audiences, and watch what happens to the conversation. In my experience it gets shorter and warmer, and it stops being about money almost at once, because the money has been dealt with.
