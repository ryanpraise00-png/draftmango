How to quit a job you're good at.

Being good at something is the strongest handcuff there is. Here's how to loosen it slowly, and leave well.

Isla Bennett12 Oct 2026

Career Change7 min read

Callum was the best analyst on his team. He was also quietly miserable. When he first came to me, he said something I hear every month: “If I were bad at it, leaving would be easy.”

He was right. Being good at a job you've outgrown is its own kind of trap. The praise keeps coming, the pay keeps rising, and every review makes leaving look a little more foolish. So you stay another year, and then another.

Why good is harder to leave than bad

When a job is going badly, the decision makes itself. When it's going well, you have to choose against evidence, and most of us aren't built for that. Three things usually hold people in place:

  • Identity. “I'm the person who's great at this.” Leaving feels like losing a piece of yourself.
  • Sunk cost. Ten years of training, and a voice that says you'd be wasting it.
  • Other people's pride. A parent, a partner or a manager who loves the version of you that stays.

None of these are reasons to stay. They're reasons to go slowly.

You're not wasting ten years. You're taking them with you.

Test before you leap

The biggest mistake I see is the dramatic exit: a resignation on a Friday and a plan on the Monday. The people who change well run small experiments first, while the salary still arrives.

  1. Talk to three people who already do the work you're curious about. Ask what a dull Tuesday looks like.
  2. Do a small piece of it on evenings or weekends: a short course, a volunteer shift, one paid project.
  3. Notice your energy afterwards, not just your interest. Tired and happy is a good sign.

Try this · 10 minutes

The two-column week

For one working week, keep a page with two columns. On the left, write down every task that gave you energy. On the right, every one that drained it. On Friday, circle the three that surprised you.

You're not looking for a new career on that page. You're looking for a direction.

Build a runway, not a cliff

Money is the fear behind most of the other fears, so name it. Work out the lowest monthly figure you could live on for six months, then decide how many of those months you want saved before you go. For most of my clients, that number is smaller than they feared.

Leave well

You're good at this job, so leave like it. Give generous notice, hand over properly, and thank the people who taught you. The industry you're leaving is smaller than you think, and the bridge you keep may be the one you need.

Callum left eight months after our first session, for a junior developer role and a pay cut he'd planned for. He still says the hardest part wasn't the leaving. It was believing he was allowed to.
