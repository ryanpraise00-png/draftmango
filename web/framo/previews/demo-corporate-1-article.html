Why the Board Should Own the Baseline

Most programmes that disappoint were never measured against where they started. Agree the measures on day one, and sign them.

Elena MarchettiManaging Partner

  • 2 Oct 2026
  • 5 min read

In This Brief

Share

Eighteen months into a transformation, a chair asks a simple question: what has it delivered? The room goes quiet. Not because nothing changed, but because nobody can say what things looked like before it began. The programme had milestones, a budget and a steering committee. What it never had was a baseline the board had agreed to.

We see this more often than any other failure, and it is the cheapest one to prevent. It costs one meeting in the first week. Skipping it costs the argument at the end.

The Argument You Lose at the End

Without an agreed starting point, every result becomes a negotiation. Finance points to a kind market. Operations credits a new hire. The sponsor remembers the old numbers as worse than they were; the sceptics remember them as better. All of them are being honest, and none of them can be proved wrong.

The cost is not only political. A programme that cannot show what it has moved cannot show what to stop, either. Money keeps flowing to the workstreams that feel busy, while the two or three that actually shift the numbers get no more attention than the rest.

What a Baseline Actually Is

A baseline is not a dashboard, and it is not a target. It is a short record of where the business stands today on the handful of measures the programme exists to move, written down before anyone starts work and signed by the people who will be judged on them.

Three properties matter far more than precision:

  • Owned. Each measure has one named executive who agrees the starting figure is fair.
  • Sourced. Every number comes from a system the business already trusts, not a spreadsheet built for the programme.
  • Fixed. Once signed, it does not move, even when it turns out to be unflattering.

Why the Board, Not the Programme

Programmes are tempted to set their own baselines, and it is easy to see why: they hold the data and the deadline. But a starting point chosen by the people who will be measured against it is a forecast, not a record. Boards do not need to calculate the numbers. They need to see them, question them and minute them, so that the record belongs to the organisation rather than to the project.

Five Measures, Signed in Week One

In practice we ask for no more than five measures. Fewer is better; beyond seven, boards stop reading. For most programmes the list looks something like this:

  1. One outcome a customer would notice, such as the time it takes to settle a claim.
  2. One financial measure the CFO already reports, such as cost to serve.
  3. One measure of capacity, such as the hours lost to rework each week.
  4. One risk measure the audit committee already watches.
  5. One leading indicator that should move within the first ninety days.

Each gets a starting figure, its source, its owner and the date it was taken. That is the whole document. It fits on one page, and it should.

The discipline is in what gets left out. Most leadership teams arrive with twenty candidate measures, each with a sponsor who would like to see it on the page. We ask one question of every one of them: if this number moved and nothing else did, would the board call the programme a success? Very few survive it, and the ones that do are the ones worth signing.

Holding the Line When the Numbers Disappoint

The real test comes three months in, when the first readings arrive and one of them has gone the wrong way. The temptation is to revisit the baseline: the data was incomplete, the season was unusual, the definition was too strict. Sometimes that is even true. It is almost never worth it.

A moved baseline teaches everyone that the record is negotiable. A held one, with an honest note on why a measure slipped, does the opposite. It tells the organisation that results will be judged the way they were promised, and that is the moment a programme earns the trust it needs for the harder decisions still to come.

When a Reset Is Justified

There are fair reasons to restate a baseline: an acquisition, a change in accounting policy, a measure that proves impossible to collect. When it happens, the board should approve the restatement the same way it approved the original, and keep both on file.

What the board should never do is let a restatement arrive quietly inside a monthly report. If the starting point changes, everyone who signed it should know why.

Where to Start on Monday

If your programme is already running without a baseline, it is not too late. Pick the three measures that matter most, take today's figures from systems you trust, and bring them to the next board meeting for sign-off. It will feel late. It is still far better than arriving at the end with nothing to compare against.

Then put that page at the front of every steering pack, in the same format, every month. The value of a baseline compounds: by the third report, nobody argues about where things began, and the conversation moves on to what to do next.

The boards we admire most treat this as housekeeping, not ceremony. One page, five numbers, five signatures. Then they get on with the work.
