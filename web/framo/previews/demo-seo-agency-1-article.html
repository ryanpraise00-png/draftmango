How to get cited in AI answers: what 400 prompts taught us.

Nadia Ferrer Strategy Lead · Oct 6, 2026 · 9 Min Read

For six months we asked three answer engines the same 400 buying questions every week and logged every source they named. The pattern that came back is plainer than the hype suggests, and most of it is work you already know how to do.

Why the citation is the new click

In 2019, 62 of every 100 searches in our client panel ended on a classic result. In 2026 it is 34. Another 27 end on an answer, and 39 end with no click at all.

The demand did not go anywhere. People still ask. They read the reply above the results and move on. When that reply names you, you have been recommended before anyone compared tabs. When it names a rival, the comparison is over before it starts.

What we tracked

The 400 prompts came from 22 clients in software, retail and healthcare. Each one was a question a buyer would really ask: which tool suits a team of ten, what a fair price looks like, who is open late nearby. We ran the set every week and recorded three things: whether the client was named, whether its page was cited as a source, and which other sources stood beside it.

An answer cited four sources on average. That is the whole shelf. There is no position eleven to be patient in.

Three things the cited pages share

  1. They rank already. Of the pages that were cited, 71% also sat in the top ten organic results for the same question. Answer engines lean on the pages search already trusts, so classic SEO is the entry ticket, not the old way.
  2. They answer first. Pages that gave a direct answer within their first 60 words were cited 2.3 times as often as pages that opened with background.
  3. They are specific. A price, a number of days, a named limit. Pages with a figure a model could lift were quoted. Pages that stayed general were paraphrased without credit.

An answer engine does not reward the best page. It rewards the page that is easiest to quote.

Mentions matter as much as pages

The surprise was how often a brand was named without its own site being a source. In 38% of the answers that recommended a client, the citation pointed somewhere else: a buyer guide, a comparison, a forum thread. The model had learned the brand from other people's pages.

That is digital PR doing a job it was never measured on. Coverage that says what you do, in plain words, on sites the engines read, moves how often you are recommended.

What did not matter

Three things we expected to count and could not find in the data: page length, publishing frequency, and markup written specially for AI crawlers. Long pages were cited when they answered early and ignored when they did not.

Where to start this month

Pick the twenty questions closest to a sale. Ask them yourself and write down who is named. For each one where you are missing, check whether you rank in the top ten. If you do not, that is the first fix. If you do, rewrite the opening so the answer comes first and carries a figure. Then look at the other sources cited and ask which of them could fairly mention you.

Run the same twenty again in a month. Lumaway did exactly this with its category question and went from absent to cited first in ten months. It is slower than an advert, and it does not switch off when the budget does.
