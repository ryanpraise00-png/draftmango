What a feasibility study tells you before you buy the land

Four weeks, a zoning summary and two massing options can save a year. What we look at, and when we tell clients to walk away.

Jonah Reyes Partner, Architect
Published
September 18, 2026
Reading Time
6 minutes

Most of the clients who call us about a site have already fallen for it. The view, the old barn, the price. A feasibility study is the four weeks in which we try, kindly, to talk them out of it, and are glad when we cannot.

What we look at

A study starts on the site, not at the desk. We walk it at three times of day, note where the sun sits in winter, where water runs after rain and what the neighbors can see. Then we read the rules.

  • Zoning: what can be built, how high, how far from each line
  • Code: what the building has to do for fire, access and energy
  • Utilities: well and septic, or a costly run to the road
  • Ground: slope, rock and wetland setbacks
The best sites are rarely the obvious ones. The worst are almost always the cheapest.

Two shapes, not one

We never show a single answer. Two massing options, drawn at the same scale on the same survey, make the trade-offs plain: a long low house that hugs the slope, or a compact tall one that keeps the meadow open.

When we say walk away

About one study in five ends with that advice. Usually it is the septic test, sometimes a setback that leaves a sliver of buildable land, once a right of way nobody had mentioned. Each time the client spent a few weeks of fees instead of a year and the price of the land.

The other four go on to a brief, a concept and, often, a building. The study becomes the first chapter of the drawing set.
