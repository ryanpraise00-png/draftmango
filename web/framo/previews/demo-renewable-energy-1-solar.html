Solar at Framo

Flat land, strong sun, a line nearby.

We build solar where the ground is level, the sky is clear most of the year and a substation sits within a few miles. Most of our newer plants pair it with batteries, so the noon sun still counts at seven in the evening.

Agrivoltaics

Sheep do the mowing.

A solar plant is still pasture. Local graziers bring their flocks in spring and keep the grass short under the panels until the first snow.

  • A grazier is paid per head

    Usually a neighbor's flock. We pay for the grazing; they keep the wool and the lambs.

  • Native seed under the rows

    Prairie grasses and wildflowers hold the soil and bring the pollinators back.

  • Rows wide enough to farm

    Twenty feet between rows, so the land can go back to crops the year the lease ends.

A Tracker’s Day

East at dawn, west by supper.

Each row turns on one axis, following the sun from sunrise to sunset — about a quarter more power than panels fixed in place.

  1. 6:30 a.m.

    Tilted 52° east

    The rows wake facing the sunrise.

  2. 12:45 p.m.

    Flat

    Peak output; the batteries charge.

  3. 6:15 p.m.

    Tilted 52° west

    The last light, as the evening load climbs.

  4. Hail or high wind

    Stowed

    The operations center turns every row to safety in two minutes.

Questions

Before the first post goes in.

Ask us anything else at a county open house — there is one before every permit.

Solar glass is made to absorb light, not reflect it. A glare study checks every road and home nearby, and we plant screening where it helps.
The posts are driven, not set in concrete, and the ground under the rows rests in grass for thirty years. Most farmers get back better soil than they leased.
They go to a recycler that recovers the glass, aluminum and silver. The decommissioning bond covers removal of every post and cable.
Only the inverters hum, and only by day. We place them in the middle of the site, so at the fence line you hear the wind.
