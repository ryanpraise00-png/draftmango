What a guaranteed maximum price really guarantees.

The GMP is the most quoted number on a CM at-Risk job and the least understood. Our VP Preconstruction on what sits inside it, and who keeps what is left.

Jennifer ColeVP Preconstruction
Topic
CM at-Risk
Read
6 min

An owner signs a guaranteed maximum price and hears “guaranteed”. A contractor signs it and hears “maximum”. Both are right, and the job goes well when both know exactly what the number holds.

Three things inside the number

A GMP is the cost of the work, plus our fee, plus a contingency for what the drawings do not yet show. The cost of the work is open book: every subcontract, every invoice and every hour of our own crews is yours to audit.

The contingency is ours to manage and yours to see. On Mercy Ridge it was 3% of the cost of the work. We reported every draw on it in the monthly cost report, with the reason, and asked before we touched it for anything larger than $25,000.

What it does not cover

Changes in scope. If the owner adds a floor, a room or a finish after the GMP is signed, that is a change order, priced the same open way. What a GMP does cover is our mistakes, our buyout and the gaps between the drawings that an experienced builder should have seen.

“A guaranteed maximum price is only as good as the estimate under it. That is why we start pricing at the first sketch.”

Who keeps the savings

On our contracts, savings under the GMP go back to the owner. Some contractors split them; we think a shared-savings clause rewards padding the number. Mercy Ridge Health got $1.1 million back at closeout.

  • Ask to see the contingency log every month.
  • Ask how allowances are reconciled, line by line.
  • Ask who keeps the buyout savings, and put the answer in the contract.

If your contractor answers all three without blinking, you have a GMP worth signing.
