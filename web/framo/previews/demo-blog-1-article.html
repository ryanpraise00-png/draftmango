Letter No. 214 · Home

The kitchen table is the only office I need.

Why I gave the desk away, what lives in the one drawer, and the 7 a.m. hour that pays for the whole day.

Elin FairlieMar 14, 2027 · 8 min read
Share

For four years I had a desk. It was a good desk, oak, from a house clearance in Leith, and it sat in the corner of the bedroom facing a wall. I wrote almost nothing at it. I wrote the letters, all of them, at the kitchen table, with the kettle in reach and the window to my left.

In January I gave the desk to a friend who has a garden shed and a novel to finish. The bedroom got its corner back. I got permission, finally, to stop pretending.

One table, three jobs

The table is 140 centimeters long and does three things a day. Breakfast until eight. Work until one. Dinner from seven. The trick is that each job ends with the table cleared, so the next one starts from nothing. It takes four minutes, and it is the closest thing I have to a commute.

Clearing is the whole system. If I leave the laptop out over lunch, I eat lunch at work. If the bread board stays out past nine, the morning never quite starts.

A desk tells you to work. A table asks what you would like to do next. From my notebook, January

What lives in the drawer

The table has one shallow drawer, and everything I need to work fits in it. That is the rule: if it does not fit, it does not stay.

  • One notebook for drafts, numbered on the spine. I am on number 31.
  • Two pencils and one pen, the pen a fountain pen I am trying, slowly, to deserve.
  • Index cards for recipe tests: weights on the front, what went wrong on the back.
  • The laptop and its charger, which go in last and come out first.

The drawer, the table at seven, and the first draft of this letter.

The 7 a.m. hour

Most of what you read here is written between seven and eight, before email, with the second cup of tea. One hour is not much. But it is an hour nobody else wants, and after six years it adds up to 214 letters and most of a cookbook.

  1. Kettle on, window open a crack, whatever the weather.
  2. Read yesterday’s page out loud. Cross out a third of it.
  3. Write until the tea is cold, then stop, even mid-sentence.

Stopping mid-sentence is the best advice I was ever given. Tomorrow starts with something to finish instead of a blank page.

Try it for a week: clear the table completely between every meal and every job. Notice which thing you most want to leave out. That is the thing that has been running your day.

What I miss about the desk

Honestly? A door. On days when the flat is full, the table is in the middle of everything, and I go to the library on Raeburn Place instead. That is fine. The desk was never the door I needed; it was just furniture I was walking past.

See you on Sunday,
Elin
