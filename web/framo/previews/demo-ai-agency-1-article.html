Where a human should stay in the loop.

An agent can prepare almost anything. Three kinds of decision should still wait for a person.

Mark JensenPrincipal Engineer, Framo

  • Sep 10, 2026
  • 5 min read

In This Note

What Should Wait 01 Prepare, Then Approve 02 Choosing the Gates 03 What to Log 04 Removing a Gate 05

0% Read

Every agent we put into production stops somewhere and waits for a person. Where it stops is the most argued line in the design. It tends to be drawn twice: too early in the pilot, when nobody trusts the agent, and too late after launch, when everybody does.

This is how we draw it once.

Three decisions that should wait

A person should hold a decision when it is costly, irreversible or about people.

  • Costly. A mistake is worth more than the minute it takes to check: a refund above a set amount, a payment to a new supplier, a discount outside the price list.
  • Irreversible. It cannot be quietly undone: an email sent to a customer, a record deleted, a contract cancelled.
  • About people. It changes someone's job, care or standing: a claim declined, a candidate rejected, a complaint closed without an answer.

Everything else is preparation, and preparation is where an agent earns its keep.

Agents prepare, people approve

The useful split is not between easy work and hard work. It is between getting a decision ready and making it. An agent can read a claim and its six photos, check the policy, compare fourteen past claims and draft the assessment. The adjuster then opens one screen: what the agent found, what it proposes and why, and two buttons.

That screen is the real product. If approving takes as long as doing the work, the gate has saved nothing, and people will either route around it or approve without reading.

How to choose the gates

List every action the agent can take and ask two questions of each. What does the worst plausible mistake cost? Can we undo it within a day? An action that is cheap and reversible never waits. One that is expensive or permanent always does.

The middle is where thresholds live. A refund waits above $500. A reply waits when the customer has written three times. An invoice waits when its amount differs from the purchase order.

Write the thresholds down as rules, not as a feeling in the prompt. A rule can be tested, versioned and argued with.

What to log

Each approval is a record, and the best evaluation data you will ever get. Log what the agent proposed, the evidence it showed, who decided, what they decided and how long it took. Log edits apart from approvals: a proposal approved after a rewrite is a miss, not a hit.

Three numbers come out of that log: the share of proposals approved unchanged, the median time to decide, and the decisions a person reversed later. Review them every month with the people who do the approving.

Keep the log where the work already lives, in the ticket or the ledger entry, so an auditor reads each decision next to its outcome.

A Test We UseIf you cannot name the person who approves an action, the agent should not be taking it yet.

When to remove a gate

A gate is a hypothesis about risk, and the log tells you when it has been answered. We remove one when three things hold for a full quarter: more than 98% of proposals pass unchanged, nothing was reversed afterwards, and the approver agrees. The action then moves to “sometimes waits”, with one case in twenty still sent for review.

Removing a gate is itself a decision that waits: it is costly to get wrong, so a person signs it. And gates come back. A new product line, a policy change or a different model restores the gate until the numbers return.

The aim is not fewer people in the loop. It is people in the loop only where their judgement changes the outcome.
